## 1. United States — Midterms: Democrats retake Congress as Trump pays the price for the Iran war

**After a historic election night, the opposition has seized both the House of Representatives and the Senate. The president admits that the conflict with Tehran weighed heavily on the vote.**

On Tuesday 3 November American voters handed the Republicans one of the clearest midterm defeats in recent history: the Democrats won the House of Representatives and took seven Senate seats from them, reaching as far as Texas, which had not elected a Democratic senator since 1988. Donald Trump himself concedes that the war against Iran, launched in February and increasingly unpopular, was a major factor in the defeat, a view shared by his vice-president, JD Vance. Without a majority on Capitol Hill for the last two years of his term, the president will have to deal with an opposition that controls the budget and the power to investigate, while his defence secretary, Pete Hegseth, already faces an impeachment resolution filed by a Republican lawmaker.

**The number :** 7 — Senate seats gained by the Democrats

### A vote on the war

Midterm elections usually go against the president’s party, but the scale of the 2026 defeat owes much to one factor. Launched with Israel on 28 February, the war against Iran has dragged on without a clear victory and sent fuel prices soaring as the Strait of Hormuz closed. Polls showed it growing steadily less popular, and the Democratic wave reached states long held by Republicans, such as Texas. The question now is whether the rebuke will change the White House’s approach to Tehran, while talks brokered by Qatar remain overshadowed by the threat of new strikes.

## 2. Iran — Washington and Tehran: fears of a return to war after the US vote

**Qatar is trying to secure an interim deal before the United States resumes its strikes. Eight months into the conflict, the Strait of Hormuz remains almost closed and oil is above $100 a barrel.**

After three days of talks in Doha, the Iranian delegation returned to Tehran without a deal, and messages between the two capitals now pass only through the Qatari mediator. The two sides remain far apart: Washington wants commitments on Iran’s nuclear programme before making any concession, while Tehran makes reopening the Strait of Hormuz conditional on lifting oil sanctions, unfreezing its assets abroad and ending the fighting in Lebanon. American strikes have never fully stopped: the island of Qeshm was hit again on 2 November, and Donald Trump, who had promised not to launch a new offensive before the elections, has not ruled out further operations.

**The number :** $107 — the price of a barrel of Brent crude on 3 November

### Hormuz, Tehran’s economic weapon

Before the war, about a fifth of the world’s oil passed through this narrow channel between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula. Since late February the Revolutionary Guards have attacked or mined ships they have not cleared, and the International Energy Agency speaks of the biggest disruption in the history of the oil market. Part of the Gulf’s crude now bypasses the strait by pipeline, but freight and insurance costs have soared: Brent, which came close to $130 in mid-October, was still trading at $107 on 3 November, against about $72 before the conflict. This is the lever Tehran means to cash in at the negotiating table.

## 3. Ukraine — Russia strikes civilians and the power grid in Ukraine ahead of new talks

**A Russian strike on a school where humanitarian aid was being handed out has killed at least 43 people in the Zaporizhzhia region, as Moscow keeps pounding the power grid. Ukrainian, Russian and American delegations are due to meet in Abu Dhabi on 10 and 11 November.**

On Monday 2 November, nine Russian aerial bombs hit an underground school in Komyshuvakha, in the Zaporizhzhia region, while humanitarian aid was being distributed; by Thursday the death toll had reached 43, according to President Volodymyr Zelensky, and dozens more people may still be trapped under the rubble. Meanwhile, drones and missiles are hitting power stations and substations in nine regions, a campaign which, according to the Institute for the Study of War, an American think-tank, aims to make each winter more painful for civilians in order to force Ukraine to give ground. It is against this backdrop that the three delegations will meet in Abu Dhabi to discuss post-war security guarantees, de-escalation and humanitarian measures, after three fruitless rounds of talks earlier this year.

**The number :** 43 — killed in the Russian strike on a school in Komyshuvakha

### NATO’s eastern flank under strain

The war is now spilling over onto the borders of the Atlantic Alliance. Poland says it foiled a Russian provocation on the night of 2 to 3 November, after a string of incidents since the summer, including the incursion of a Russian military helicopter in September. American intelligence agencies consider a limited attack on Poland or the Baltic states, designed to test NATO’s cohesion, to be possible, which Moscow denies. In October, NATO’s Supreme Allied Commander Europe presented a more offensive doctrine, built on a “drone wall” and deep strikes into Russian territory in the event of aggression.

## 4. Israel — In Israel, Gadi Eisenkot wins the backing of most MPs against Benjamin Netanyahu

**Abandoned by the ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism party, Benjamin Netanyahu gathers only 46 recommendations, against 67 for his rival, who is backed by the Arab parties. In Gaza, a year after the ceasefire, Israeli strikes still claim lives almost every day.**

At the end of consultations held by the president, Isaac Herzog, on 4 and 5 November, 67 of the Knesset’s 120 members recommended Gadi Eisenkot to form a government: the opposition’s 48, joined by the two Arab parties, Raam and the Joint List, with 19 seats between them. Benjamin Netanyahu, whose camp won 53 seats on 27 October, now musters only 46, after the ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism declined to recommend anyone without guarantees on military-service exemptions for yeshiva students. The head of state is expected within days to ask Mr Eisenkot, who says he wants to form a “Zionist government”, to build a coalition within 28 days, extendable by 14, with the Joint List pledging to back him from outside government.

## 5. Sudan — In Sudan, a drone war is strangling civilians

**More than three years into the conflict, the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces are stepping up strikes on infrastructure. The crisis is now spilling over into neighbouring Ethiopia.**

The Rapid Support Forces (RSF), at war with the regular army since April 2023, seized the Sali military base in Blue Nile state on 3 November and are using drones against power stations and depots, leaving hundreds of aid lorries stranded for lack of fuel. A year after the fall of El Fasher, the capital of North Darfur, Amnesty International is still documenting executions, detentions and torture there, and describes a city emptied of the vast majority of its inhabitants. The Sudanese authorities accuse Ethiopia of hosting RSF drone launch sites; Addis Ababa hit back on 4 November by expelling six Sudanese diplomats, raising fears that the conflict could spread across the Horn of Africa.

**The number :** 13.5 million — Sudanese displaced, at home or abroad

### A war out of sight

With more than thirteen million people displaced, Sudan is living through one of the worst humanitarian crises on the planet, yet the country remains almost closed to foreign journalists and the world’s attention is elsewhere. The conflict pits two former allies against each other: General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, head of the army, and Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, known as “Hemedti”, leader of the RSF, who together ousted the civilian government in 2021. Both sides are accused of war crimes, and Britain has just sanctioned fifteen of their commanders. The UN envoy, Pekka Haavisto, hopes to bring the RSF to the table before Christmas to discuss a transition to civilian rule.
