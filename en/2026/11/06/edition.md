## 1. Iran — Tankers hit by mines in Hormuz as Iran sets seven conditions for Washington

**Tehran says at least five tankers were damaged in the Strait of Hormuz on Thursday, as the Pentagon stands ready for a resumption of fighting. Through Qatar, Iran has passed a proposal on its nuclear programme to the United States.**

On Thursday 5 November Iranian forces reported at least five tankers and a tugboat ablaze in the Strait of Hormuz, most of them after striking mines on routes Tehran had not authorised; Britain’s UKMTO agency also reported a vessel hit by two projectiles north of Qatar. The same day, a senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran had sent Washington, through Qatar, a proposal on its nuclear programme, to be discussed only once seven demands are met, among them the lifting of sanctions, the unfreezing of Iranian assets and war reparations. The United States, which is studying the text, wants the nuclear issue on the table from the outset, and thousands of American troops stand ready to resume fighting, according to the New York Times, just as several of Donald Trump’s allies distance themselves from the war after the defeat of 3 November.

**The number :** 5 — tankers damaged in the Strait of Hormuz on Thursday, according to Tehran

### Seven conditions to reopen the strait

On a visit to Russia on Thursday, the senior Iranian security official Mohsen Rezaei summed up Tehran’s line: until its seven conditions are met, the Strait of Hormuz will stay shut and there will be no negotiation. Iran is betting on the weight of a passage through which about a fifth of the world’s oil normally flows, and the Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, ruled out on Wednesday any "inclination to submit". In Washington the political mood has shifted: the vice-president, JD Vance, openly blames the electoral defeat on the war, the Republican senator Ted Cruz says it has gone on too long, and the Democrats, who will control Congress from January, want it ended.

## 2. Paraguay — Paraguay’s Senate opens Santiago Peña’s impeachment trial after his bid to resign fails

**Congress was unable to accept the president’s resignation on Thursday after his allies walked out, leaving it without a quorum. Senators began his trial on Friday, in his absence, over the collapse of the digital bank Ueno.**

On Friday 6 November Paraguay’s Senate opened the impeachment trial of the president, Santiago Peña, who failed to appear, and voted by 39 to 3 to reject his request for another 72 hours to prepare a defence entrusted to the lawyers Ricardo Preda and Adolfo Ferreiro. The day before, his Colorado Party allies had walked out of a joint session of Congress, lacking the votes to accept a resignation that would have earned him a life seat in the Senate with immunity; the charges include his former stake in the holding company of Ueno, the bank seized on 3 November. Removal requires 30 of the 45 senators and would hand power to the vice-president, Pedro Alliana, until August 2028; the defence says it may challenge the process before the Supreme Court and international bodies, while the chief of staff, Javier Giménez, and the interior minister, Enrique Riera, have resigned.

**The number :** 30 of 45 — senators’ votes needed to remove Santiago Peña

### A banking scandal at the top of the state

Born out of a former finance company, Ueno had become the country’s digital bank of choice, with more than 70 branches. Its collapse cut off a large share of the population from their accounts, apps and cards overnight. The deposit-guarantee fund is due to repay up to about $36,000 per customer, which would cover 97% of depositors, according to the Associated Press. Eight executives have been detained, including the founder, Miguel Vázquez, and 27 people have been charged with breach of trust and attempted fraud. The attorney-general has opened three criminal investigations, which also target current and former officials, among them the president, who has apologised to Paraguayans and says he will co-operate.

## 3. Ukraine — Ahead of the Abu Dhabi talks, Russian and Ukrainian envoys meet at the White House

**Melania Trump brought together envoys from both countries in Washington on Thursday, with no progress announced. On the ground, Moscow is targeting Ukraine’s energy and logistics networks while Kyiv steps up drone attacks on Russian refineries.**

On Thursday 5 November the American first lady, Melania Trump, received Vladimir Putin’s envoy, Kirill Dmitriev, and Russia’s human-rights commissioner at the White House, along with two Ukrainian officials, including the head of the president’s party, David Arakhamia, and urged them to look beyond a mere "temporary ceasefire". The White House credits her with brokering a six-day truce in Oleshky, in the occupied Kherson region, which allowed nearly 800 civilians to be evacuated; the Russian strike of 2 November on an aid distribution in Komyshuvakha, which killed 43 people according to Kyiv, came the day after it expired. The strikes go on: Moscow says it hit a depot of the Nova Poshta delivery company near Kharkiv and a cargo ship off Odesa, while more than 200 Ukrainian drones targeted the Ryazan region, ahead of three-way talks due in Abu Dhabi on 10 and 11 November.

**The number :** ≈ 800 — civilians evacuated from Oleshky during a six-day truce

### An energy war

Each side is now going after the other’s economy. According to Al Jazeera’s Moscow correspondent, Ukraine has stepped up its strikes on Russian refineries, hitting Yaroslavl two days before Ryazan, where the governor, Pavel Malkov, counted more than 200 drones in a single night. Russia, for its part, is targeting electrical substations and logistics hubs, and Kyiv has endured rolling blackouts since mid-October as winter approaches. Melania Trump’s humanitarian diplomacy runs alongside the main negotiating channel, that of the American envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, who spoke to Volodymyr Zelensky ahead of the Abu Dhabi meeting.

## 4. Israel — Herzog to name Israel’s next prime minister on Sunday, with Gadi Eisenkot in pole position

**With 67 of the 120 members of the Knesset behind him, the former army chief awaits the mandate the president is due to hand out on 8 November. In Benjamin Netanyahu’s camp, down to 46 backers, some cry "stolen" election.**

After two days of consultations, Israel’s president, Isaac Herzog, called the outcome "clear", with 67 of 120 members recommending Gadi Eisenkot against 46 for Benjamin Netanyahu, but announced on Thursday 5 November that he would keep talking and would not award the mandate to form a government until Sunday. That tally rests on an unprecedented alliance between the mainly Jewish opposition bloc and the two Arab parties: Ra’am, whose leader, Mansour Abbas, says he wants to enable an "emergency government", and the United List, which would back a minority government from outside. In the outgoing camp positions are hardening: a minister from the far-right Otzma Yehudit party told the head of state that the election had been "stolen", although the electoral commission found no irregularities, and the new Knesset is due to be sworn in on 10 November.

**The number :** 67 — members of the Knesset recommend Gadi Eisenkot for prime minister

### Betting on a Jewish-Arab majority

Never before has a candidate for prime minister leaned so openly on the Arab parties, which together hold a record 19 seats. Without them, Gadi Eisenkot’s bloc has only 48 members, well short of the 61 required. Likud has attacked the alliance, with its minister Yoav Kisch accusing the Arab parties of dedicating their victory to the people of Gaza, while Mansour Abbas wants an end to "loyalty tests" for his community. The outcome also hinges on the ultra-Orthodox: United Torah Judaism, which recommended no one, wants the exemption of yeshiva students from military service written into law, and Avigdor Lieberman, leader of Yisrael Beiteinu, says he is open to sitting with it, according to Channel 12.

## 5. Somalia — Somalia’s Supreme Court rules the extension of the president’s term unlawful

**The country’s highest court on Thursday gave the parties thirty days to reach agreement and ninety days to hold elections. The day before, the head of state, Hassan Sheikh Mohamud, had sacked the chief justice.**

On Thursday 5 November the constitutional chamber of Somalia’s Supreme Court ruled that the constitutional amendments adopted in March could extend neither the term of President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud, which expired on 15 May, nor that of parliament, and confined the federal government to a caretaker role. The day before, the president had sacked the chief justice, Bashe Yusuf Ahmed, who was hearing an opposition challenge, and replaced him with a close ally, Ibrahim Idle Suleiman; the court declared the dismissal void, and the former prime minister Hassan Ali Khaire accuses the government of sending soldiers into the courthouse. The judges demand an agreement between political parties within thirty days and elections within ninety, whereas the president meant to lead the country to its first direct elections, planned for April and May 2027, a reform whose terms the opposition and several regional parties dispute.

**The number :** 90 days — the deadline set by the Supreme Court for holding elections

### A political crisis amid an insurgency

The crisis could hardly come at a worse time for the country’s security. Al-Shabaab, which is linked to al-Qaeda, controls large swathes of territory, and donors are cutting funding for the international military mission: Uganda, the biggest contributor to the African Union force, announced this week that it would withdraw all its troops by the end of 2027, and Washington has stopped funding a UN logistics mission, according to AFP. The UN’s political mission in Somalia closed on 31 October. The dispute over the length of the president’s term had already led to clashes between factions in Mogadishu in June. Hassan Sheikh Mohamud champions a reform meant to replace the clan-based electoral system with universal suffrage.
