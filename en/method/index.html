Method

Diurna is a short daily briefing on world affairs: five stories every morning, chosen by cross-checking the front pages of the major newsrooms, and a globe to place them on.

ACTA·DIVRNA

Why Diurna

In 59 BC, Julius Caesar, then consul, had the Acta Diurna, “the daily acts”, posted every day in the public places of Rome: political decisions, trials, births, marriages and deaths. Copied and passed on, they were read, the historian Tacitus reports, as far away as the provinces and the legions. They are often described as the first newspaper in history.

Diurna picks up the idea two thousand years later: every day, the world explained in three sentences.

How stories are chosen

A story makes the edition when at least three leading newsrooms run it at the top: Reuters, Associated Press, AFP, the BBC, Le Monde, The Economist, Al Jazeera, The New York Times, the Financial Times, France 24, The Guardian or NPR.

Priority goes to events that change the situation within the week: a vote, a decision, fighting, a negotiation, a deadline. Long-running trends wait until an event brings them back to the fore.

The fifth story, “Out of the spotlight”, is kept for a serious crisis that gets little coverage in the European press: Sudan, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the Sahel, Haiti, Myanmar, Yemen. It rotates from week to week.

How it is written

Each story takes the form of a short article: a headline, a standfirst, a three-sentence paragraph that sets out the facts, explains the causes and looks ahead, then an analysis. Depending on the kind of news (conflict, election, economy, diplomacy, disaster, society), the page adds modules: casualty figures, places, results, each side’s demands, charts.

Figures, accusations and claims are attributed to those who make them. A module only appears when its data is sourced. The English edition is written for English-speaking readers in its own right, not translated word for word from the French.

The data

Reference figures come from public sources, collected automatically every morning: the World Bank (population, inflation), the European Central Bank (exchange rates), the US Energy Information Administration (oil prices), the USGS and GDACS (natural disasters). Every module shows its source and date.

How it is made

The edition is prepared every morning at around 7am Paris time, in French and then in English, and revised in the evening if a major event occurs. Selection and writing are done by an artificial intelligence model (Claude, developed by Anthropic), following a public editorial charter. Where a fact is in doubt, the sources linked under each story take precedence.
